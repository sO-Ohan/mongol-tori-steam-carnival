# Mongol-Tori — school talk (class 5–10)

Speaker script for `school.html`. Target run time **19 minutes**, then at least ten minutes of
questions. Press **N** in the deck to read these notes on your laptop; **T** starts the clock in
the bottom rail.

The talk is built to be interrupted. The `ASK` / `DO` lines are the point of it — a room of
thirteen-year-olds will give you thirty seconds of politeness and then decide whether this is
happening *to* them or *with* them.

## Before you go

- Take the rover if you possibly can. Slide 06 is the natural place to break and go outside.
- If you cannot take the rover, take *something* they can hold: a wheel, a motor, a board, a radio.
- Bring a controller. Someone in that room should drive something before you leave.
- Know one local thing they can join on Sunday — a club, a teacher, a contest. Name it on slide 16.
- The laptop copy works offline. School projectors and school wifi are not friends.

---

## 01 · Title
`0:00 – 1:00 · 60s`

> "Assalamu alaikum. We are Mongol Tori — মঙ্গল তরী, the Mars Chariot. We build Mars rovers at BRAC University, and this year ours finished seventh in the world."

  - Say the Bangla name first, loudly. It is their name too.
  - Then the line that matters: **"every person on this team is a student."**
  - Don't read the slide. Let the photo do the work for a beat.

**ASK THE ROOM:** "Hands up — who here has ever opened up something to see what was inside?" Wait
for the hands. Tell them that is the entire job.

## 02 · The astronaut
`1:00 – 2:00 · 60s`

> "This is an astronaut. He has been to space. He came to Dhaka, walked into our lab, and asked us how our robot works."

  - Silence for two seconds before you speak. Let them look at the flight suit.
  - Point at the student doing the explaining: **"that is a student. Same age as your older brother or sister."**
  - Land it: "Nobody in this photo was famous. They just built something worth flying across the world to see."

## 03 · Who we are
`2:00 – 3:10 · 70s`

> "In 2015 a few students decided to build a Mars rover. They had never built one. Nobody had shown them how."

  - Six sub-teams: metal, electronics, code, radios, biology, self-driving. Say them fast — the point is the variety, not the list.
  - For the students who do not like maths: **there is a job on this team for a biologist, an artist and a writer too.**
  - Finish on the amber line: you start with curiosity, not with skill.

## 04 · This is not Mars
`3:10 – 4:10 · 60s`

> "Look at this place. It is not Mars — it is a desert in Utah, in America. It is the closest thing to Mars we have on Earth."

  - 116 teams entered. 38 got to the desert. We finished 7th. Say the funnel slowly, one number at a time.
  - Worth saying out loud: **teams from Bangladesh beat teams from countries with space agencies.**

**ASK:** "How far away do you think Mars is?" Take two guesses before you move on — then tell them
it changes, because both planets keep moving.

## 05 · Why robots go first
`4:10 – 5:20 · 70s`

> "Why send a robot at all? Because Mars will kill you in about fifteen seconds."

  - Minus sixty degrees. No air you can breathe. A message takes up to twenty minutes to get there — so you cannot shout "stop!" and expect it to help.
  - **Be clear that these four numbers are Mars, not Utah** — you were standing in Utah one slide ago. The last line of the slide does the switch for you: Utah in June is about +35 °C, and that is where we actually test.
  - If anyone asks: −60 °C is the NASA average for Mars; it swings from about +20 °C to −153 °C.
  - The last number is the important one: **zero mechanics.** Whatever you send has to look after itself.
  - Bridge to the rover: "So it has to be a machine that can think a little on its own. That is what we build."

## 06 · Meet Taurus
`5:20 – 6:40 · 80s`

> "This is Taurus, this year's rover. About as heavy as two of you, and it has to do four completely different jobs."

  - Walk the four cards with your hand on the screen: ears, arm, brain, wheels. One sentence each.
  - The wheels always get a reaction: **no air inside them, so there is nothing to puncture.**
  - Don't go deeper than the four labels. Detail is for the questions afterwards.

**IF YOU BROUGHT THE ROVER:** stop here, go outside, and come back for the rest. This slide is the
natural break.

## 07 · The four jobs
`6:40 – 7:50 · 70s`

> "Four missions, thirty minutes each. Dig. Deliver. Repair. Think."

  - Dig — soil, tested on the spot, for signs that something once lived.
  - Deliver — find a toolbox, pick it up, carry it over rocks to an astronaut.
  - Repair — switches, valves and cables, with a robot hand, on a broken lander.
  - Think — drive to a point on a map and back with nobody steering.
  - Keep it moving. This slide is a list; the next one is the story.

## 08 · The hardest rule
`7:50 – 9:00 · 70s`

> "Here is the rule that makes it hard. The driver is not allowed to see the rover. They sit in a tent, a kilometre away, and drive it through one camera."

  - **Move your mouse.** The bright circle is all the driver gets — the rest of the desert is invisible to them. Hunt around the slide for a second; the room will lean in.
  - Say it plainly: "Imagine driving a car looking only through a straw. That is the competition."
  - Every design decision in this talk comes from this one rule.

**ASK:** "Would you rather drive it, or be the one who wrote the code so it drives itself?" Both
answers are the right answer — that is the point.

## 09 · Radio
`9:00 – 10:00 · 60s`

> "So how does the rover hear us? The same way a radio hears a station — invisible waves, right through the air, right through a hill."

  - Three radios at once. One of them *will* fail on the day. The other two bring the rover home.
  - 1.2 km with hills in the way — that is our worst case, and the worst case is what we quote.
  - Get their hands up: they are standing in radio waves right now and cannot feel a thing.

## 10 · Driving itself
`10:00 – 11:20 · 80s`

> "Close your eyes and try to walk to the door of this room. You would count steps, feel the wall, guess. That is exactly what the rover does."

  - Four senses, each one wrong in its own way: GPS wanders, wheels spin on sand, the balance sensor drifts, cameras get confused on flat ground.
  - The computer listens to all four and decides who to trust.
  - Land the last line — **"four imperfect answers, one good one — that is most of engineering."**

**OPTIONAL:** pick one student, ask them to close their eyes and point at the door. They will be a
little off. That is drift.

## 11 · Make it, or ask
`11:20 – 12:30 · 70s`

> "The main circuit board of the rover did not exist anywhere, so we designed it ourselves. We call it Kacchim."

  - Then the antenna story: the ones we could buy were not good enough, so we wrote to a company in Finland — strangers, seven thousand kilometres away, with no reason at all to answer a student email.
  - **They answered.** Pause there. This is the most useful lesson in the talk for a fifteen-year-old.
  - "The worst thing that happens when you ask is nothing."

## 12 · It breaks
`12:30 – 13:40 · 70s`

> "Now the part nobody puts on Facebook. This thing breaks constantly."

  - 2023: the main circuit burned out in a desert camp, hours from any shop — rebuilt by hand in four hours.
  - 2024: the body snapped in the middle of a mission, in front of the judges — fixed in six hours with what was in the toolbox.
  - Say the closing line straight to the students at the back: **if your project fails, you are not bad at this, you are doing it.**

## 13 · The scoreboard
`13:40 – 14:40 · 60s`

> "This is every year we have competed. Look at the shape of it — it goes down as often as it goes up."

  - Trace the whole line with your hand. Don't stop on any one year and don't call a year the bad one — those were real people, and a dip is an accident on the day, not a measure of the batch.
  - If a student asks about the low point, answer kindly and generally: **"a part failed during a run. It happens to every team, and the ones who come back are the ones who stayed."**
  - Then go straight to the recovery: seventh in the world, out of 116 teams.

## 14 · Drones
`14:40 – 15:30 · 50s`

> "We build drones as well. Ours flies ahead of the rover and reports back what is over the hill."

  - The point to make: solve the hard problem once — knowing where you are — and it works in the air and on the ground.
  - The Navy came to our lab to see it, and students did the explaining. Keep it short.

## 15 · Your turn
`15:30 – 16:40 · 70s`

> "And then we do this — we take the rover to a school ground and hand the controller to somebody your age."

  - Slow right down. This is the slide they remember.
  - Point at each photo and name what is happening. The third one always gets a laugh — yes, it holds a person's weight.
  - Finish with why you are actually standing there: someone let us touch a robot once, and that is the only reason we are here.

## 16 · How to start
`16:40 – 18:10 · 90s`

> "You do not need a lab. You need five things, and you can start every one of them this week."

  - Read the five headings only — open something broken, learn one skill properly, make it move, find two friends, enter something.
  - The one to dwell on is number three: **the first time something you built moves on its own, you are done for.**
  - End on the amber card: ask the question. Tell them you answer every message a student sends.

**TELL THEM WHERE:** name the school club, the teacher, or the contest they can actually join on
Sunday. Vague inspiration fades by lunch.

## 17 · Close
`18:10 – 19:00 · 50s`

> "মঙ্গল তরী — Mars Chariot. Come and ask us anything. What broke, what it cost, how to join, whether we have ever set something on fire. Yes, we have."

  - Say তুমিও পারবে out loud, in Bangla, and mean it.
  - Then stop talking and take questions — leave at least ten minutes.
  - Questions that always come: how much does it cost · how fast does it go · can it fly · did you meet aliens · what marks do I need to join a team like this.
