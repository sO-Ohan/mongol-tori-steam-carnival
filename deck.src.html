01 / Who we are

A student team at
BRAC University

Mongol Tori started in 2015. We design and build Mars rovers — machines that drive over rough ground, pick things up, test soil, and find their own way to a point on a map.

Everyone on the team is a student. Seniors graduate every year, so a big part of the work is teaching the next batch everything we learned before we leave.

02 / Why we do it

We are two things at once

Entity one

A research group

We work on self-navigation, mapping and localisation — the problem of a machine knowing where it is without anyone telling it. The same work goes into drones and into ground robots that can be useful here, not only on Mars.

Our aim is to design things at industry standard, in Bangladesh, with parts we can actually get.

Entity two

A competition team

Competitions give us the one thing a university project usually lacks: a fixed date, a written specification, and judges who do not care how hard it was.

If the rover does not work on the day, we score zero. That pressure is what turns a design on a screen into hardware that survives a desert.

And one more thing we take seriously: being a place where the next group of students can learn this. Most of us joined because a senior showed us a rover once.

06 / The rover

Taurus

One machine that has to drill rock, plug in a cable, climb a hill and then drive itself home. Everything is a compromise between those four jobs.

  • Four-wheel rocker-bogie, so every wheel stays loaded on broken ground
  • Steel frame, aluminium electronics bay, 3D-printed wheel grips
  • 7-axis arm mounted on a sliding rail, so it reaches without getting heavier
  • Three radio bands live at the same time

Drawn in SolidWorks · stress-tested in simulation before anything was cut

08 / Breakthrough two — the antennas

We stopped using
the antennas in the box

A radio's range on the datasheet was measured somewhere that is not our test field. So we contacted CompleTech in Finland, the company behind ComAnt antennas, and asked if their engineers would look at our problem.

They did. We sat in calls with them and worked through it together: we simulated our own testing grounds, they designed antennas for that specific case, and we measured the result in the field instead of trusting a number.

  • On the rover2×2 MIMO omni, plus a Miniflex for the 433 MHz link
  • At the base2×2 MIMO sector antenna on the mast
  • Checked byRF simulation of the actual site, then a real range test

They had no reason to help a student team in Dhaka. They helped because we asked a specific technical question. That is worth knowing if you are starting out.

09 / Breakthrough three — knowing where it is

Four sensors, each wrong in a different way

GPS wanders. Wheels slip, so counting rotations lies. An IMU drifts as it adds up its own small errors. Stereo cameras get lost on flat sand. Used together, their mistakes cancel out.

SBG SystemsInertial unit — motion and attitude, fast
GNSSGlobal position, trusted less when satellites are poor
ZED 2i stereoDepth and a 3D point cloud of the ground
ArUco markersExact fix whenever a known marker is in view
→
One position

A Kalman filter merges all four into a single best guess of where the rover is, and how sure it is.

→
One map

A 360° scan at the start is matched against a map we built beforehand, so it knows the terrain around it.

→
One path

The planner picks a route to the goal and keeps re-checking it against whatever appears in front.

All of it runs onboard on a Jetson Orin Nano. We test the whole stack in simulation first, because a bug found in software costs an afternoon and a bug found in the desert costs a mission.

10 / Breakthrough four — our own boards

The parts we could not buy, we made

Our controller

Kacchim

Kacchim is the board we designed to sit between the computer and everything that moves. It drives the motors, reads the sensors, runs the science payload and carries the wireless emergency stop.

One board across the whole rover means one thing to learn, one spare to carry, and one place to look when something misbehaves at midnight.

Our power board

Six rails, hot-swappable

A backplane with one plug-in card per voltage. If a rail dies in the pit, you pull the card and push in a spare instead of rewiring the rover.

GaN switching gets it above 95% efficient, every rail has its own fuse, and it reports live voltage and current back to the base station.

Where we do buy, we buy well. The arm joints use MyActuator servo drives — the same class of actuator used on precision electronics assembly lines — and they back the team as a partner.

11 / Not only rovers

We build drones too

The rover does not work alone. Our drone flies ahead of it during a mission, looks at ground the rover cannot see yet, and stays on the same radio network as the rover and the base station.

The navigation problem is the same one: a machine working out where it is and what is around it, without a person driving. Solving it once pays off twice.

The Bangladesh Navy came to our lab to see the drone work. Students explained it to them at the table, which is roughly how everything here starts.

12 / Outreach
